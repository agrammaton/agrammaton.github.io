## Bio

I am Associate Professor in the Department of Electrical Engineering at the NUST School of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science (SEECS) in Islamabad.

I graduated with a BS in Electronics Engineering from GIK Institute of Engineering Sciences and Technology, followed by a Masters of Engineering Science from the University of New South Wales (UNSW). I did my PhD, also from UNSW, under the supervision of Vijay Sivaraman and Diet Ostry. My research consisted of devising lightweight security solutions for bodyworn healthcare devices.

I have about three years industry experience, working at WorldCALL Broadband Ltd., in various engineering roles. As a researcher, I’ve worked at CSIRO and UNSW on projects including green WiFi, software-defined networks, and data forensics, and, more recently at Newcastle University, in Feng Hao‘s group, where I worked on secure election protocols and cryptocurrencies.

Beyond academia, I advise government and civil society organizations on technology policy, digital transformation, and institutional development. I also write regularly for local media and contribute to the *In Plato's Cave* podcast.


## Research

My academic work has focused primarily on computer networks, information security, electronic voting, and emerging technologies. More specifically, I have worked on questions surrounding blockchain and cryptocurrencies, the Internet of Things, machine learning, and technology policy.

My research lies at the intersection of **technology, institutions, and society**.

A central theme of my work is the relationship between technological systems and the institutions within which they operate. I am particularly interested in how technologies such as electronic voting, blockchain, artificial intelligence, and networked information systems reshape questions of trust, security, accountability, and governance.

My work in **electronic voting and election technology** examines the technical, institutional, and political challenges involved in designing trustworthy digital electoral systems. This includes questions of security and privacy, voter confidence, system design, and the institutional conditions necessary for technology to contribute meaningfully to democratic processes.

I am also interested in **information security and computer networks**, particularly where technical vulnerabilities intersect with human behaviour and institutional structures. More broadly, I study emerging technologies not simply as engineering artefacts, but as systems embedded in social and institutional environments.

An increasingly important part of my research concerns **technology policy and emerging technologies**. Here I am interested in how governments and institutions can respond intelligently to rapidly changing technologies while preserving security, accountability, innovation, and public trust.

Across these areas, my broader objective is to understand not only **what technologies can do**, but also **how they change the institutions and societies in which they are deployed**.
